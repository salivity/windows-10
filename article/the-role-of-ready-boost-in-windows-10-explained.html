The Role of ReadyBoost in Windows 10 Explained

ReadyBoost is a built-in Windows feature that allows users to use external flash memory—such as a USB flash drive or an SD card—as an auxiliary disk cache to improve system responsiveness. While initially introduced in Windows Vista, ReadyBoost remains available in Windows 10, primarily serving as a performance bridge for systems equipped with traditional mechanical hard drives and limited random-access memory (RAM). This article details how ReadyBoost functions within Windows 10, when it delivers measurable benefits, and why its role has diminished in modern computing.

At its core, ReadyBoost functions as a middle tier of storage cache, positioned between the high-speed system RAM and the slower primary storage drive. Windows 10 identifies frequently accessed system files and application data, encrypts them, and caches them onto the connected flash drive. Flash memory possesses significantly faster random read access times than mechanical hard disk drives (HDDs), meaning the operating system can retrieve scattered, small-file data much quicker from a USB drive than waiting for the read/write heads of an HDD to physically locate the files.

For the feature to function, the host system must meet specific requirements. The external storage device must offer sufficient throughput—generally at least 2.5 MB/s throughput for 4 KB random reads and 1.75 MB/s throughput for 1 MB random writes—and possess a capacity between 256 MB and 32 GB per device (with support for multiple devices). Windows 10 configures ReadyBoost directly through the "Properties" menu of the compatible removable drive.

The practical impact of ReadyBoost in Windows 10 depends heavily on the computer’s underlying hardware. On low-end systems equipped with traditional HDDs and 4 GB or less of RAM, ReadyBoost can alleviate bottlenecks during startup, file searches, and application launching by handling peak random-read workloads.

However, ReadyBoost plays virtually no role on modern, well-equipped hardware. If Windows 10 is installed on a solid-state drive (SSD), the operating system automatically disables ReadyBoost. Because SSDs offer internal read/write speeds and random access latencies vastly superior to those of external USB flash memory, utilizing a USB drive as a cache would degrade performance rather than enhance it. Additionally, systems with 8 GB of RAM or more rarely exhaust their memory space for routine caching, rendering external flash caching unnecessary.

Consequently, the role of ReadyBoost in Windows 10 is primarily that of a legacy support feature. It acts as an inexpensive, temporary performance aid for aging, budget-tier PCs relying on mechanical hard drives, rather than a universal performance booster for modern machines.
